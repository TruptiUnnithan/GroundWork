A ramp-up system for a new PM's first 90 days

Turn “can you look into X” into scoped, agreed work in two days.

Groundwork is one private Loop workspace, a handful of Copilot prompts and a fixed daily and weekly rhythm. It turns scattered, undocumented team knowledge into things you can act on. It runs on sanctioned Microsoft tools, takes one afternoon to set up and needs no engineering.

Why it exists

As an intern you got scoped tasks. Now you get ambiguous asks.

Much of what matters on a product team is tacit or undocumented. Left alone, that means a slow ramp-up, tasks that drift or get redone, and a first Connects cycle where impact is hard to show. Each gap below has one goal and a date.

Task execution

No reliable method for going from a one-line ask to a finished outcome: who to talk to, what to read first, what done looks like.

Run any task to done without being told how. Every new task starts with a one-page brief, agreed with the requester within two working days.by day 60

Vocabulary

Codenames and engineering terms (rings, flighting, ship room) slow you down in meetings, and asking in a group feels costly.

Follow every meeting you attend. No more than one unknown term per meeting, counted from your debrief notes.by day 45

Craft

You don't yet know what a good spec looks like on this team, or how design and prototyping fit the cycle.

Write a spec the team accepts in two review rounds or fewer.by day 90

Engineering literacy

You can't yet follow how a feature moves from PR to production, so you can't judge timelines or push back credibly.

Trace one shipped feature end to end and talk it through with the engineer who owned it.by day 60

People

You don't know who owns what, who to learn from, or who could mentor you.

Know the people. A map of at least 15 people across eng, design and PM, 8 or more 1:1s held, one mentor agreed.by day 75

Voice

You hold back opinions and keep no record of what you've contributed.

Show your impact. A wins log with evidence links, ready to paste into Connects without reconstruction.every Friday

The task playbook

Seven steps, scaled to the size of the ask.

Every task goes through the same steps. The point is to catch misunderstandings before they cost a week. Pick a task size to see which steps apply.

Task size

All seven steps. Full brief, agreed in writing.

  1. 1

    Capture the ask exactly · skip

    Write down the words used, who asked, when, and any deadline mentioned. Don't interpret yet.

  2. 2

    Find the why · skip

    Ask the requester three questions, in person or in one message. What decision or outcome does this feed? Who else cares about the result? What does good look like, and by when? If they can't answer, that's a finding too: the task may not be ready.

  3. 3

    Map what exists · skip

    Search for prior work before doing anything new: Copilot across email, Teams, SharePoint and Loop, plus ADO for related work items. Note the two or three names that keep appearing.

  4. 4

    Talk to two people · skip

    One who knows the history, one who will be affected by the outcome, often an engineer or designer. Ask what's been tried, what constraint nobody writes down, and what they'd worry about.

  5. 5

    Write the one-page brief · skip· five lines in a chat message

    Send it to the requester: “Here's how I'm reading this. Anything wrong before I start?” This one message prevents most redone work.

  6. 6

    Do the work in visible slices · skip

    Share a rough draft at about 30% done, not 90%. Early feedback is cheap; late feedback is a rewrite.

  7. 7

    Close the loop · skip

    Send the result with a two-line summary of what changed and what's open. Log it in the Wins page with the link. Add new terms and people to Glossary and People.

Stuck for more than half a day on any step? The question goes to the Questions bank and gets asked at the next 1:1 or in a short direct message. Being stuck silently is the failure mode, not asking.

Engineering literacy

One feature trace beats any course.

Pick one feature that shipped recently and follow it end to end. Then ask the engineer who owned it for 30 minutes: “What went wrong, and what would you change?”

Specs, design and prototyping

Learn the house style before you write.

Ask your manager for the two or three best specs from the past year and turn their shared structure into your template. Find where design work lives (Figma, design reviews, the design system), ask the designer on your area when PMs should join, and sit in on one design review as an observer before contributing. Learn which stage uses a wireframe for discussion and which a prototype for testing.

People and mentors

Two rings of 1:1s, then one specific ask.

Weeks 1–4: your direct engineering, design and PM counterparts. Weeks 5–8: people two steps out, chosen from names that keep turning up in playbook step 3. Each 1:1 carries one question you want answered and one about them.

By week 6, pick the person whose judgement you trust most and ask for a monthly 30 minutes with a purpose, such as “getting better at pushing back on scope”. Specific asks get yeses.

Voice and work style

The Friday review, answered in writing.

  1. What did I move forward this week? Link it.
  2. Where did I have an opinion and not say it?
  3. Who should I meet next week, and what do I want to learn?

Question 2 builds voice. After four weeks, look for the pattern in which meetings, people and topics you go quiet on, then pick one setting a week to speak up in, with the point prepared.

Work style

Protect the thinking time.

Note when you do your best thinking and block two deep-work slots a week. Note how your manager prefers updates (async message, 1:1 or written doc) and match it.
